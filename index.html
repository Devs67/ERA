Phased rollout

Either model can be staged. You don't write one large cheque — each phase proves itself before the next begins.

  1. 1 Term onePrimary, mobile

    Grades 1–5 run on mobile kits inside existing classrooms. No infrastructure required, and the first parent-facing win arrives fastest.

  2. 2 Terms two and threeMiddle school lab

    The dedicated room is commissioned — electronics benches, 3D printers, secure storage. Grades 6–8 come onto the programme.

  3. 3 Year twoFull fabrication

    Capstone studio, competition pipeline, student portfolios and demo days. Grade 9 and the Grade 10 capstone year.

What the school provides

Deliberately light. Nothing here is specialist equipment — and for Grades 1–5, nothing here is new.

Space

A standard classroom is enough. Grades 1–5 can use an existing room between other lessons; Grades 6–9 need it dedicated, so kits and half-finished builds can stay out.

Tables and chairs

Basic furniture, arranged so students can work in threes around a shared surface. No benches or specialist workstations needed to start.

One device per group of three

Laptop, desktop or tablet. It must be Bluetooth enabled — most kits pair wirelessly — with a USB port for boards that flash over cable. A class of thirty runs on ten devices.

Power and Wi-Fi

Sockets within reach of each group, and internet access. Almost every software tool runs in a browser, so device specifications can stay modest.

Lockable storage

A cupboard in or near the room. Kits have small parts and need to be counted back in at the end of every session.

A protected timetable slot

A regular weekly period that doesn't get borrowed. The single biggest predictor of whether a programme survives its first year is whether it has a fixed place in the timetable.

InfrastructureGrades 1–5 need no dedicated space. Grades 6–9 need a room with power points, ventilation for printers, and lockable storage.

Scope to confirmConsumables, filament, replacement parts and annual maintenance sit inside Option B and outside Option A. Both are priced separately in the proposal.

Pilot firstOne grade band, one term, one room. A pilot converts more often than a full-lab decision, and it costs the school very little to say yes to.

Then, every module

Learn it. Build it.
Then solve something real.

Every module follows the same three beats — in Grade 5 and in Grade 9. Children always know where they are in the arc, and so do their parents.

  1. 01 BootcampFirst session

    The technology on its own terms — what it is, what it does, and how to make it do something before the session ends.

  2. 02 ProjectsTwo to three builds

    Guided builds that turn a demonstration into a skill. Each one adds a constraint the last one didn't have.

  3. 03 Design-thinking challengeOne per module

    An open real-world problem. Students define it, ideate, prototype and test — with no single right answer waiting at the back of a book.

Every module ends with something a child can hold up and explain. Grade 5 runs 7 modules a year, rising to 10 by Grade 9 — two projects and one challenge inside each one.

What comes out of it

Per module

Two to three finished project builds and one design-thinking challenge solution, each documented by the student.

Per year

Between 14 and 20 project builds, plus 7 to 10 challenge solutions — evidence a parent can actually see.

Per stage

A portfolio that grows with the child, ready for showcases, competitions and senior-school applications.

Why the challenge mattersThe projects teach the tool. The challenge is where students meet an open problem with no answer key — the part that transfers out of the lab.

Same shape, rising difficultyA Grade 5 challenge and a Grade 9 challenge follow the same design-thinking arc. What changes is the complexity of the problem and how much scaffolding is removed.

Who teachesYour teachers do. We certify them on every module before it runs, and they deliver it. Experion does not take the class.

Quality checksWe observe a live session mid-module, score it against the same rubric your teachers were trained on, and send leadership a written report — what is landing, what is not, and what to change before the next module.

What we believe

01

The process comes first. The tool comes second.

A child who can define a problem, sketch three ways to solve it and deliberately test the worst one is already doing engineering — whether they are holding a soldering iron or a handful of building blocks. We teach design thinking from Grade 1 and let the technology get harder around it. That order never reverses.

02

A consumer becomes a maker in one specific moment.

The LED lights. The robot turns. The app speaks back in their own voice. There is a visible before and after in a child's face when something they built actually works, and everything that comes later is easier because of it. Our job is to engineer that moment early, and then again every few weeks.

03

Using technology well has to be taught.

Between the build and the challenge sits a question children are almost never asked: should this be made at all? Who does it help, who does it leave out, and what does it cost the person using it? A child who has trained a model and watched it fail on somebody unlike themselves understands bias better than any lesson can explain it.

Which is why every module, in Grade 1 and in Grade 8, has the same three beats.

  1. BootcampOne session. The technology on its own terms.
  2. ProjectsTwo to three guided builds. Each adds a constraint.
  3. ChallengeOne open problem. No answer key at the back.

Nine years of it, from Grade 1 to Grade 9, with nothing taught twice.
